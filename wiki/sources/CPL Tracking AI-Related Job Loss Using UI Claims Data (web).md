# CPL Tracking AI-Related Job Loss Using UI Claims Data (web)

## Citation

Hyman, Ben, Till von Wachter, Diana Herrera, Roozbeh Moghadam, Jacob Morris, Swapnil Motghare and Kara Segal. "Tracking AI-Related Job Loss Using Unemployment Insurance Claims Data in California." California Policy Lab (CPL), June 2026. https://capolicylab.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Tracking-AI-Related-Job-Loss-Using-Unemployment-Insurance-Claims-Data-in-California.pdf. Read 2026-11-11.

## Bottom line

- The report introduces the California AI-Unemployment Tracker (CAIT). Through May 2026, statewide unemployment insurance (UI) claims show no surge from AI-exposed occupations.
- Claims rose and stayed high for college-educated workers in high-exposure jobs, in the San Francisco Bay Area and in technology sectors. The authors say this is descriptive and not proof that AI is the cause.

## Background

CPL is a research institute at the University of California. It made the report with the Labor Market Information Division of the Employment Development Department (EDD). The Russell Sage Foundation and the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation supported the work.

## Key claims

- "Since the release of ChatGPT-3.5 in 2022, statewide UI claims through May 2026 show no evidence of a surge in AI-related layoffs." (Key Findings, p. 4)
- "From 2023–25, 30.3% of initial California UI claims were filed by workers in occupations that had high AI exposure prior to their job loss." (Key Findings, p. 4)
- "Eligible workers with sufficient prior earnings and who were laid off through no fault of their own, can receive weekly UI benefits up to 50% of their prior weekly earnings (up to $450 per week) for up to 26 weeks." (Section I, p. 5)
- "However, the analysis is descriptive and cannot causally disentangle tech sector layoffs after the COVID-19 remote-work boom from emerging AI-related labor market pressures." (Conclusion, p. 19)
- "This work has been supported (in part) by grant #2510-51111 from the Russell Sage Foundation and with support from The Alfred P. Sloan Foundation" (Acknowledgements, p. 20)

## Evidence and method

A research report from administrative data. Each UI claimant selects the occupation of the job before the layoff. The authors match that occupation to two AI exposure measures. The potential measure is from Eloundou et al. (2024, in Science). The observed measure is from Claude use (Handa et al., 2025, Anthropic Economic Index). The report compares trends before and after late 2022, by education, region and industry. CPL acknowledges The James Irvine Foundation and the Woven Foundation as supporters.

## Limits

The authors list these limits (Section IV, p. 18):

- Exposure measures do not show that a workplace adopted AI.
- UI claims miss workers who do not file, for example the self-employed, gig workers and workers who find a new job quickly.
- Post-pandemic changes in the technology sector can also explain the rise.
