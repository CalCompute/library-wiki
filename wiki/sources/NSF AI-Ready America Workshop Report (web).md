# NSF AI-Ready America Workshop Report (web)

## Citation

SeedAI. "AI-Ready America: A two-day workshop conducted by SeedAI to develop recommendations on accelerating AI diffusion, access, and adoption for all Americans." Workshop report, 29 pages, with support from the U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF Award No. 2608403) and the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation. Workshop held January 29-30, 2026; publication date not stated. Hosted by NSF. https://nsf-gov-resources.nsf.gov/files/AI-Ready_America_Workshop_Report.pdf. ## Bottom line

- About 100 experts met in Washington, D.C., in January 2026. They said how the United States can build the institutions for AI diffusion, access and adoption.
- The report's first nation-scale recommendation is to "Build and Grow AI Research and Compute Commons". The commons would use state and regional hubs in trusted public institutions, with federal seed money that draws state and local money.
- It names Empire AI and the Massachusetts AI Hub as two state models. It says the practitioners who train others, not end users, are the best investment.

## Background

SeedAI is "a Washington, D.C.-based nonprofit focused on national AI readiness". It ran the workshop with NSF and Sloan Foundation support. Participants came from federal agencies, state and local governments, industry, philanthropy, higher education and civil society.

The sessions were state and local coordination, nation-scale efforts, domain-specific strategies and AI literacy. The report says its views "do not necessarily reflect the views of the U.S. National Science Foundation or the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation" (p. 1).

## Key claims

- "Federal investment should support a shared AI compute and research infrastructure accessible to community colleges, universities, and civil society organizations. The National Artificial Intelligence Research Resource (NAIRR) pilot demonstrates how coordinated access to compute, data, models, and training resources can broaden participation in AI research and workforce development." (R1, p. 15)
- "These compute commons should leverage state and regional hubs embedded within trusted public institutions including land-grant universities and their Extension networks, regional public universities, community colleges, and SBDCs with federal seed funding structured to catalyze matching state and local investment." (R1, p. 16)
- "In New York, Empire AI—a ten-institution consortium focused on GPU supercomputing—represents a unique, large-scale compute-first strategy for supporting AI research infrastructure. The Massachusetts AI Hub demonstrates a slightly different approach. Rather than centering primarily on compute, the Hub is designed to pair expanded access to computing and shared data with support for research collaboration, startup growth, and small business adoption". (Session Three, finding 5, p. 19)
- "The primary bottleneck to nation-scale AI adoption is not end users but practitioners and intermediaries—'super nodes'—that shape how others engage with technology: teachers rather than students, nurses rather than patients, small business advisors rather than business owners." (Session Two, finding 3, p. 14)
- "Participants described technology companies attaching exclusivity conditions to institutional partnerships, such as providing funded equipment or tools on the condition that institutions not partner with competitors." (Session Two, finding 5, p. 15)
- "One particularly useful indicator of AI readiness identified by workshop participants is access to data and compute. NSF should support a scoping study that evaluates compute and data capacity gaps across the country, evaluates shared infrastructure models, and develops recommendations to close these gaps." (Roadmap, item 4, p. 28)
- Principle 1: "Build on existing infrastructure—don't duplicate it." Principle 7: "Structure public-private partnerships around outcomes." (Statement of Principles, pp. 25-26)

## Evidence and method

A workshop report. The method section says the findings come from "transcripts of all table discussions, presentation materials from lightning talks and keynotes, and participant survey responses" (p. 7). It used thematic analysis. Working groups ran under the Chatham House Rule.

A draft went to some participants and outside experts for comment. Christine Kirkpatrick of the San Diego Supercomputer Center is among the commenters (p. 29).

## Limits

The report is expert opinion from one two-day meeting, not a study with measured outcomes. It gives no cost figures for a compute commons. It mentions no California program. The workshop participant list is public, but the report does not say who said what.
