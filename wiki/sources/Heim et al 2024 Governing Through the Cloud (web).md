# Heim et al 2024 Governing Through the Cloud (web)

## Bottom line

- The paper says that compute providers should have legal obligations in AI regulation. They can act in four roles: securers, record keepers, verifiers and enforcers.
- It says that providers already hold non-confidential data that shows a workload's type and the amount of compute it uses.
- It uses U.S. Executive Order 14110 as a case study and says that the scheme needs international adoption.

## Background

The authors are at GovAI, the Center for a New American Security, the Institute for Progress, the Harvard Kennedy School and the University of Oxford. The paper is a whitepaper on arXiv, version 2 of March 26, 2024. It looks at frontier AI only. Its references include a February 2024 release of Senator Scott Wiener on legislation for large-scale AI systems in California. California's Government Operations Agency cites the paper in its November 2026 report ([GovOps AI Safety in 2026 and Beyond (web)](GovOps%20AI%20Safety%20in%202026%20and%20Beyond%20%28web%29.md)).

## Key claims

- "We argue that compute providers should have legal obligations and ethical responsibilities associated with AI development and deployment, both to provide secure infrastructure and to serve as intermediaries for AI regulation." (Abstract)
- "Compute providers can play an essential role in a regulatory ecosystem via four key capacities: as securers, safeguarding AI systems and critical infrastructure; as record keepers, enhancing visibility for policymakers; as verifiers of customer activities, ensuring oversight; and as enforcers, taking actions against rule violations." (Abstract)
- "we describe how non-confidential information, to which compute providers largely already have access, can provide two key governance-relevant properties of a computational workload: its type—e.g., large-scale training or inference—and the amount of compute it has consumed." (Abstract)
- "At a minimum, providers have access to billing information and can access basic technical data on how their hardware is used." (Executive Summary, Technical Feasibility, p. 4)
- "Via Executive Order 14110 the US government is already beginning to implement recordkeeping roles for compute providers by requiring them to implement a Customer Identification Program (essentially a Know-Your-Customer (KYC) program) for foreign customers, and to report foreign customer training of highly capable models to government." (Executive Summary, Constructing an Oversight Scheme, p. 5)
- "The intent of the proposed regulatory frameworks is not to encompass the entirety of compute providers' customer base indiscriminately." (Section 1.2, p. 9)
- "Our discussion is not a comprehensive policy blueprint ready for implementation." (Section 1.4, p. 10)

## Evidence and method

A policy and technical analysis, not peer-reviewed. It sets out the four roles, reviews the data that providers collect, and describes technical tools for workload classification and verification. It uses Executive Order 14110 and the Department of Commerce's proposed rule as a case study. The library read the abstract, the Executive Summary and parts of Sections 1 and 4.3. It did not read Sections 2, 3 and 5 in full. The Markdown copy runs words together; the quotes follow the PDF.

## Limits

The paper is about frontier AI and large corporate customers. It does not discuss public research compute, state compute programs or CalCompute. It says that the international and legal parts are "not explored in detail" (Section 1.4). The authors wrote the paper while Executive Order 14110 was in force. The authors say that "being an author does not imply agreement with every claim made in the paper" (title page note).
