# Turing 2025 Mapping the Potential GenAI public sector work (web)

## Citation

Youmna Hashem, Jonathan Bright, Sukankana Chakraborty, Kate Onslow, John Francis, Anton Poletaev and Saba Esnaashari. "Mapping the Potential: Generative AI and Public Sector Work. Using time use data to identify opportunities for AI adoption in Great Britain's public sector." The Alan Turing Institute, June 2025. https://www.turing.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-05/ons_tus_final_report.pdf. Read 2026-11-11.

## Bottom line

- The report estimates that generative AI could support about 41 percent of public sector work time in Great Britain. That is 3.4 of 8.3 hours a day.
- The share is 49 percent in education and 33 percent in healthcare. It is 38 percent for frontline workers and 46 percent for non-frontline workers.
- The authors say that technical exposure is not the same as successful adoption.

## Background

The authors work in the Public Policy Programme of The Alan Turing Institute, at The British Library in London. The Programme wrote this report with the Office for National Statistics (ONS) and the Department for Transport. The report uses the ONS Public Sector Time Use Survey (PSTUS). It says that it "has not, however, been formally peer reviewed" (p. 3).

## Key claims

- "According to our work, approximately 41% of public sector time is spent on activities that could be supported through the use of generative AI." (Introduction, p. 6)
- "Based on our model, approximately 3.4 hours (out of the 8.3 hours) are potentially exposed to generative AI (41%)." (Results, p. 12)
- "38% of frontline workers' activities were found to be potentially exposed to generative AI, compared with 46% of non-frontline workers' activities." (Results, p. 13)
- "Public sector workers spend an average of 30 minutes per day on emails – a timeframe which, according to the methodology outlined above, could potentially be reduced by more than 70% with the use of state-of-the-art generative AI models." (Results, p. 14)
- "They illustrate that a blanket, one-size-fits-all approach to adoption across the public sector that ignores these varying dimensions would likely lead to unintended consequences, ranging from missed opportunities in some areas to disorganised uptake in others." (Discussion, p. 16)
- The method "does not, however, account for the commercial costs associated with the adoption of new technologies; nor does it explore the various other strategic factors determining successful implementation, such as the availability of internal technical capacity and expertise" (Discussion, pp. 16 to 17)
- "A forthcoming report by the project team at The Alan Turing Institute has found that only 4% of teachers report using generative AI for marking." (Discussion, footnote 5, p. 17)

## Evidence and method

A research report from a national institute. Researchers coded 80 work activities from the PSTUS with a rubric based on Eloundou et al. (2024), from E0 to E4. The authors weighted the scores by the time that workers record in their diaries. The first survey wave had 1,513 respondents and 3,005 diaries, weighted to the public sector population (p. 14, footnote 4).

## Limits

The authors say the coding is "ultimately a subjective process" (Limitations, p. 10). They say the upper-bound scoring "might in some instances over-estimate the potential for exposure" (p. 11). Part of the sample came from volunteers, so the survey oversamples some areas. The data are for Great Britain, and the report does not cover costs, compute or California.
