# Future Skills Centre Adoption Ready public sector AI exposure (web)

## Citation

Graham Dobbs, Vivian Li, Viet Vu and André Côté (The Dais). "Adoption Ready? The AI Exposure of Jobs and Skills in Canada's Public Sector Workforce." Project Insights Report, Future Skills Centre, Toronto, October 2025. https://fsc-ccf.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/adoption-ready-the-ai-exposure-of-jobs-and-skills-in-canadas-public-sector-workforce.pdf. Read 2026-11-11.

## Bottom line

- The report finds that 74 percent of Canada's public sector workers are in occupations exposed to AI, against 56 percent of the whole Canadian workforce.
- It finds that 49 percent of public sector jobs are in low-complementarity occupations, where AI is more likely to replace tasks. In the federal public sector, 58 percent of workers are in the high-exposure, low-complementarity group.
- It says that human oversight, access to tools and training, and public service values decide whether adoption succeeds.

## Background

The Future Skills Centre (FSC) is a Canadian centre that the Government of Canada's Future Skills Program funds. The Dais did the research in 2024 and 2025, with an FSC investment of $249,948. This document is a short summary of the project. It is part of a series on AI in three sectors: the public sector, arts and culture, and financial services. The public sector in the study is about 1.1 million federal, provincial and municipal employees.

## Key claims

- "The findings show that public sector workers are more likely than the broader Canadian workforce to be in AI-exposed occupations (74% versus 56%), with nearly half in low-complementarity roles where AI could substitute for tasks." (Executive Summary)
- "In particular, the federal public sector has a much higher concentration of workers in the high-exposure and low-complementarity quadrant (58%), reﬂecting a larger share of jobs in business, ﬁnance, and administration occupations than in Canada's overall workforce." (What We're Learning)
- "In the higher-complementarity quadrant, public sector workers in occupational groups such as senior management, natural and applied sciences, education, law, and government services are more likely to see AI as a tool for assistance rather than replacement." (What We're Learning)
- "Successful adoption, however, will depend as much on non-technical factors—human oversight, access to training, and adherence to public service values—as on the technology itself." (Executive Summary)
- "Responsibly managing this transition will require clear and transparent strategies, prioritization and risk assessment of immediate opportunities, and a strong commitment to engaging and supporting public sector workers—especially those in high-exposure occupations—throughout the process." (Why It Matters)

## Evidence and method

A research summary from a government-funded skills centre. The project used data from Canada's 2021 Census of Population and the National Occupational Classification (NOC). It placed more than 500 public sector occupations on two measures: exposure to AI and complementarity to AI. The method follows recent work on task-based automation risk.

## Limits

This document is a summary; it does not give the full tables or the method in detail. The full report is at https://fsc-ccf.ca/research/adoption-ready/, which this library has not read. The data are for Canada and from 2021. Exposure measures what AI could do, not what governments use. The document does not mention California.
