# WEF AI and the Future of Entry-Level Work 2026 (web)

## Citation

World Economic Forum (WEF), in collaboration with PwC. "Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Entry-Level Work: A Framework for Safeguarding and Reinventing Early Career Pathways." Insight report, World Economic Forum, June 2026. https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Artificial_Intelligence_and_the_Future_of_Entry_Level_Work_2026.pdf. Read 2026-11-09.

## Bottom line

- The report estimates that 37 percent of young workers worldwide have jobs with medium to high exposure to AI-driven task change. In Northern America the share is 69 percent.
- It gives a framework of four dimensions: job access, job design, talent pipelines and education system alignment. It asks education systems to expand work-integrated learning and alternative pathways alongside degrees.

## Background

The WEF Centre for the New Economy and Society wrote the report with PwC. It uses PwC survey and job-posting data and a WEF dialogue with over 200 leaders and experts. The report writes for employers, policy-makers and educators in all regions. The WEF says the findings do not necessarily represent its own views.

## Key claims

- "Globally, more than one in three (37%) young workers are employed in occupations with medium to high exposure to AI-driven task change, including three in four young workers in Eastern Asia (75%) and two in three young workers in Northern America (69%) and Europe (63%)." (Executive summary, p. 4)
- "68% of entry-level workers report having seen an increase in productivity due to AI, but 45% also spend more time working in general as a result of AI." (Executive summary, Finding 3, p. 4)
- "Importantly, strain on entry-level pathways predates the rise of AI." (Introduction, p. 8)
- "Education systems expand work-integrated learning and strengthen alternative pathways into employment alongside traditional degrees" (Box 1, p. 9)
- "a widely-cited analysis by Brynjolfsson et al. finding a 16% decline in entry-level jobs in AI-exposed fields in the United States since late 2022" (Finding 1, p. 11)
- "In the United States, for example, entry-level roles in the most AI-exposed occupations have seen around 40% more new skills emerge since 2019 than those in the least exposed occupations" (Finding 8, p. 32)

## Evidence and method

An insight report from an international membership organisation and a consulting firm. Its data come from the PwC AI Jobs Barometer and the PwC Global Workforce Hopes and Fears Survey 2025. The survey covers over 9,000 entry-level workers in 48 countries. The report also uses International Labour Organization (ILOSTAT) employment data and interviews with business leaders. The exposure figures map PwC occupation scores to ILOSTAT youth employment. It also cites outside labour market studies.

## Limits

The report gives regional and United States figures but no California figures. It does not separate public sector employers, except in one survey chart by sector (Figure 13). It says that the role of AI in recent hiring declines "remains contested" (Finding 1). The Markdown copy mixes the PDF's text columns, so the quotes above follow the PDF's column order.
